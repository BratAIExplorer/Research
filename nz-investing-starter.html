The receipts (go read them)

  • Vanguard studied lump sum vs spreading it out (DCA) across US, UK and Australian markets. Lump sum came out ahead roughly two-thirds of the time over the following year. DCA still beat sitting in cash most of the time. Sources differ on exact numbers (66–68%). Search "Vanguard cost averaging invest now or temporarily hold your cash" to read it yourself. I checked: the finding is real; I did not confirm the exact % on Vanguard's own page.
  • Charles Schwab compared 5 pretend investors: one with perfect timing every year vs one who just invested on day one. Perfect timing won, but only modestly. Read: Does Market Timing Work?
  • Counterpoint: investors like Robert Shiller show that high starting prices tend to mean lower returns over the next 10–20 years. So "price matters" is a real argument. It just doesn't tell you the month to buy.

5 · US-listed vs Irish (UCITS) vs NZ funds

Same shares inside, different "wrapper". The wrapper changes your paperwork and one nasty risk.

Correcting a common claim: you'll read that Irish funds "cut US dividend tax to 15%". True, but a US-listed ETF also gets 15% for a NZ resident if you file a W-8BEN form (otherwise 30%). So the withholding is about the same. The real differences are US estate tax and what's available on your NZ platform.

Read this before trusting any number above:
  • Price only, not total return (my assumption). Distributing funds (US ones, plus AGGG) pay cash out, so their price change leaves out income. Accumulating Irish funds reinvest inside, so their price includes it. That makes Irish funds look better than they are, and bonds look worse. Compare yield and price together.
  • Listing currency, not NZD. Prices are in USD, except SWDA (British pence). NZD/USD moves change what she actually gets.
  • Start date shapes the story. VT is +79% over 3 years but only +71% over 5. The 5-year window started near a market high. Never judge a fund on one window.
  • Gold and silver pay no income and just track the metal. They were the top performers here (silver +159% over 3 years, gold +121%). That is exactly when people rush in. Silver swings far more than gold. Treat them as a small spice, not the meal. Whether a gold/silver fund counts as a FIF is a question for an accountant.
  • ETC, not UCITS: the Irish-side gold and silver products are "ETCs", a different wrapper from a UCITS fund. Check who issues it and where.
  • Copper: CPER tracks copper futures (my understanding; confirm), not metal in a vault. ICOP holds copper mining companies, a different bet, and is a young fund with no 5-year record. The Irish copper ETC had no return data in my source.
  • Platinum is rarer than gold and tied to car and factory demand. It has lagged gold and silver here.
  • Energy (XLE, IUES) is up about 180% over 5 years, but that window started at an oil slump. It swings with oil prices.
  • Japan and Korea: EWJ and SJPA are Japan only. EWY is South Korea only, up 134% in one year, which is a flashing yellow light, not a green one. I assume Korea's market leans heavily on big chip makers, so it may not spread you away from tech. Check top holdings. I found no Irish-listed Korea fund in my data.
  • One-country funds (Japan, Korea) are riskier than whole-world funds. "Global" = VT, VWRA, ACWI and the SPDR ACWI fund above.
  • Spread ≠ safe. Holding different things helps, but in a crash many things fall together. Property (VNQ) did only +7% over 5 years here.
  • Past is not a forecast. The last 3 years were strong for shares. Bonds went nowhere over 5.
  • Tax columns are rough. "Up to $50k" = yield × rate, before any credit for the 15% US tax already withheld. "Over $50k" = 5% × rate, same for every fund. Bond funds may be treated differently. Ask an accountant.
  • Home (US/Ireland) is from the fund names. I did not open each factsheet. Confirm domicile there. Fees (TER) are not shown. Check each factsheet.
US estate tax in one line: if a non-US person owns more than about US$60,000 of US-situs assets (this includes US-listed ETFs) when they die, the US can tax the excess at up to 40%. I checked: the US$60k figure appears across multiple sources. I did NOT confirm whether a NZ–US estate treaty changes this. Assume it applies and ask a tax specialist if you're heading past US$60k.

5b · Keep it small: 3 to 5 funds, then stop

The list above is a menu. Nobody should order everything.

Why 3 to 5 max

  • Funds overlap. A world fund already owns the same big companies as an S&P 500 fund.
  • More funds = more fees to check, more tax paperwork, more things to watch, more urge to tinker.
  • Many people do fine with just 1 or 2.

Honest note: "3 to 5" is a common rule of thumb from index-investing teachers. I did not find a study proving it is the magic number.

A simple shape (an example, not advice)

  • Core: 1 or 2 broad funds (whole world, or US plus rest of world).
  • Safety: 1 bond or cash-like piece, if you need the money within a few years.
  • Spice: 0 to 2 small bets (gold, one country, one sector). A common rule of thumb keeps all spice to a small slice, like 5 to 10%.

Red flags in a shortlist

  • Two funds doing the same job (like VOO and CSPX).
  • Five funds all chasing the same hot theme.
  • A fund you can't explain in one sentence.
  • Buying it because it went up 100% last year.

Tip: tick funds in the table above. It counts them and warns about overlap.

How often should you review? Once a year.

Pick one fixed date. Twice a year at most. Not daily. Checking prices all the time makes people panic and sell at the wrong moment. I did not find a study proving the best rhythm. Once a year is the common default among index investors.

The 4-question yearly check

  1. Is my reason and timeline the same?
  2. Is any piece far off my plan (say more than 5 points)? If so, put new money into the smaller piece first. That is gentle "rebalancing".
  3. Are the fees still fine?
  4. Did a rule change (tax lines, limits)?

Pick your date: Near 31 March, the end of the NZ tax year. Check your total offshore cost against the NZ$50k FIF line (and whether it has changed).

Check sooner only if…

  • Your job or income changes, or you move country.
  • A goal is about 5 years away. Then slowly shift toward safer pieces.
  • A fund closes or its fees jump.
  • You need the cash.

Don't

  • Sell because of a headline.
  • Chase last year's winner.
  • Add a 6th fund "just because".

6 · NZ tax decoder

Three questions. Gives you a pointer, not a tax return.

The NZ cheat-sheet

  • No general capital gains tax. But gains can be taxed if you bought shares intending to sell them for profit. It's about intent, not just how often you trade.
  • Offshore stuff over NZ$50k cost falls under the FIF rules. Default method (FDR): you're taxed on 5% of the start-of-year value, even if the fund paid nothing.
  • PIE funds (Kernel, Simplicity, etc.) pay tax for you at your PIR: 10.5%, 17.5% or 28%. Max is 28%.
  • Transitional residents: new arrivals may be exempt from tax on most foreign income, including FIF, for about 4 years. Huge if it fits you. Ask IRD.

Things I could not confirm

  • A Chartered Accountants ANZ item dated 19 Oct 2026 says the FIF $50k threshold may rise to $100k. It's a proposal. I couldn't open the page, so I don't know if it became law. Check IRD.
  • PIR income thresholds: sources disagree (older: $14k/$48k/$70k; newer: $15.6k/$53.5k/$78.1k). Use IRD's "find my PIR" tool.
  • The FIF $50k test counts cost, not value. Whether a given holding is covered by the exemption can vary. Check the ticker.

9 · Real places to check everything

Official or independent. Not sales pages. Open them, read for 10 minutes, then decide.

Before you hand money to anyone, ask 4 things

  1. Are they on the FMA register of licensed providers?
  2. What are all the fees, in dollars per year on my amount?
  3. Where is the Product Disclosure Statement, and what's the worst year it shows?
  4. Why would they recommend it? Do they get paid if I say yes?
Red flags: "guaranteed" or "target" returns of 15–20%, a countdown timer, "limited slots", pressure to decide today. A real investment shows you the downside first. If someone pitches you a private "opportunity", including a friend's, treat it like a stranger's until you've checked their licence.
