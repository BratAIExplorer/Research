0 · The big idea: two countries, two rulebooks

SINGAPORE

Where you live

Sources say Singapore does not tax gains from long-term investing, and does not tax dividends from Singapore companies. That is friendly. But trading like a business can be taxed.

USA

The sneaky one

Singapore has no tax deal with the US. So US-listed funds can lose 30% of their dividends to the US. Plus a "death tax" risk above about US$60,000. Section 5 explains.

INDIA

Where you're from

If you buy Indian shares or funds, India can tax your gains even though you live abroad. Your passport doesn't change that. Where the investment is does.

Biggest question to answer first: will this money be used in Singapore, in India, or somewhere else? Spending in one currency while investing in another adds a hidden risk: the exchange rate.

Tap ↻

The proof (go read it)

  • Vanguard compared "invest all now" with "spread it out" in US, UK and Australian markets. Investing all now won about two-thirds of the time over a year. Spreading out still beat sitting in cash most of the time. Sources give 66–68%. Search "Vanguard cost averaging invest now or temporarily hold your cash". I checked that the finding is real. I did not confirm the exact % on Vanguard's own page.
  • Charles Schwab compared pretend investors. One had perfect timing every year, one just invested on day one. Perfect timing won, but only a little. Read it ↗
  • Other side: Robert Shiller's work says high starting prices tend to mean weaker returns over the next 10–20 years. Price matters. It just doesn't tell you the month.

5 · US-listed vs Irish funds (the Singapore twist)

Same companies inside. Different "box". The box changes how much US tax you lose.

US-listed fund (e.g. VOO)Irish "UCITS" fund (e.g. CSPX)
US tax on US dividends30% (Singapore has no US tax treaty)~15%, taken inside the fund (Ireland has a US treaty)
Can you claim it back?No treaty, so noNo, it's already taken inside
US "death tax" riskYes: above about US$60,000 of US assets, up to 40%Generally not US-situs
Singapore tax on the dividendsSources say foreign dividends to resident individuals are generally not taxed. Confirm with IRAS.
Easier to findMost brokersSome brokers. Check what yours offers
Fair challenge: the articles saying "buy Irish funds!" are mostly written by companies that sell them. The facts check out, but see how small the dollars are: S$10,000 in VOO at about 1% yield = S$100 of dividends. 30% vs 15% is S$30 vs S$15. The death-tax risk above US$60k is the bigger deal. I did not confirm whether any treaty changes that. Assume it applies.

Read before trusting the numbers:
  • Price change only (my assumption). US funds pay cash out, so their price leaves out income. "Acc" Irish funds reinvest inside, so their price includes it. That makes Irish funds look better and bond funds look worse.
  • Not in S$ or ₹. Prices are in USD (SWDA: British pence). Your result changes with the exchange rate.
  • Start date matters. VT: +79% over 3 years, but +71% over 5. Never judge on one window.
  • ES3 (Singapore's STI ETF) had no return data in my source, so I show none. I won't invent it.
  • "Home" is from fund names. Confirm on each factsheet, where you'll also find fees (not shown here).
  • Gold and silver make no income and just track the metal. They were the top performers in this list (silver +159% over 3 years, gold +121%). That is exactly when people rush in. Silver swings far more than gold. Treat them as a small spice, not the meal.
  • ETC, not UCITS: the Irish-side gold and silver products are "ETCs", a different wrapper from a UCITS fund. Check who issues it and where.
  • Spread ≠ safe. Holding different things helps, but in a crash many things fall together. Property (VNQ) did only +7% over 5 years here.
  • Past results are not a forecast.

6 · Tax decoder

Two quick tools. They point the way. They are not a tax return.

Singapore side

India side (Indian shares / equity mutual funds)

Cheat-sheet

  • Singapore: sources say no tax on capital gains for individuals investing for the long term. Dividends from Singapore companies are tax-free to you. Trading as a business can be taxed.
  • US funds: 30% US tax on dividends. See section 5.
  • India: gains on Indian shares and funds are taxed in India even if you live abroad. The fund house usually takes the tax (TDS) before paying you. You may need to file an Indian tax return.
  • Money moves: NRE and NRO are the two kinds of NRI bank accounts. They have different rules. Ask your bank.

Things I could not confirm

  • The Indian rates in the box use 20% short-term and 12.5% long-term (above ₹1.25 lakh) for listed equity, as most sources say after July 2024. One source still showed the old 15%/10%. Check the Income Tax Department.
  • Whether the India–Singapore tax treaty changes your case. Ask a tax adviser.
  • FEMA rules on moving money between India and Singapore.
  • Singapore Savings Bonds for foreigners. Two sources disagreed. Check MAS.
  • The SRS (retirement top-up) limit for foreigners. Check IRAS.
  • A 2024 rule (Section 10L) on foreign-asset gains. Sources disagree on whether it touches individuals. Check IRAS.
  • Debt funds, bonds, and ULIP/insurance products are taxed differently. The box covers equity only.

9 · Real places to check everything

Official or independent first. Broker articles are labelled, because they sell things.

Before giving anyone money, ask 4 things

  1. Is this firm licensed by MAS? (Check the MAS register.)
  2. What are all the fees, in dollars a year on my amount?
  3. What is the worst year shown in the product document?
  4. Do you get paid if I say yes?
Red flags: "guaranteed" returns, 15–20% "target" returns, WhatsApp or Telegram "tip groups", a countdown timer, "only a few slots", pressure to decide today. MAS has said scammers pretend to be licensed firms, so find the firm's number yourself and call it. A friend's private "opportunity" is a stranger's until you check the licence.

Also be careful with: savings-plus-insurance bundles. They can have long lock-ins and costs. Ask for the full cost table and what you'd get back if you stopped early.
